292,680 rows. What does one row represent, and why does it matter?

Task 1: reading it

  • One row is one review: one person rating one beer once
  • Behind the 292,680 rows: 50,826 beers, 5,841 breweries, 13,030 reviewers
  • So “reviews by style” counts a beer once per review. A style with a few famous beers can look bigger than a style with many quiet ones

The unit of observation is the first thing to settle in any dataset. Every count and every average depends on it. We change it on purpose in Task 7.

Three columns, three problems. What are they?

Task 2: reading it

  • Ratings are text: "14/20" is five characters, not a number. R cannot average text
  • beer_ABV has "-" in 14,140 rows. It means missing, not zero. Real alcohol-free beers are in the data at 0.0–0.5, so 14,140 zeros would be wrong and would pull the average down
  • review_time is a Unix timestamp: seconds since January 1, 1970. 1157587200 is September 7, 2006
  • And in beer_style: sake (12 kinds), cider, perry, and mead are not beer; Klsch and &#40;IPA&#41; are encoding damage from the scraper

None of this is visible in a chart. You find it by looking at the values before you plot anything. That is what Tasks 1–2 are for.

What is the scale of each rating? How many ABV values are missing?

Task 3: reading it

  • Non-beers removed: 3,693 rows, leaving 288,987
  • Scales differ: overall 1–20, taste and aroma 1–10, appearance and palate 1–5. A taste of 7 and an overall of 14 are both 70% of their scale; you cannot compare the raw numbers
  • 14,019 reviews have no ABV: keep them for everything about ratings, drop them only in ABV charts (na.rm = TRUE)
  • Reviews run from April 2000 to January 13, 2012. Remember that date for Task 8

Did you check the AI’s work? Mean overall should be 13.2. If yours was 0.7, the assistant kept the /20 part. The assistant cannot see your output.

Task 4: Variation Warm-up

Reviews by style

Which styles dominate? And what does “most reviewed” actually measure?

Reviews by style: reading it

  • IPA leads (18,635), then Pale Lager, Belgian Strong Ale, Imperial Stout
  • Pale Lager is the most consumed beer in the world, and it is second here only because everyone has tried one. This is what enthusiasts write about, not what the market buys. Keep that in mind for Task 9

The distribution of ABV

What shape? And what is hiding at the far right?

The distribution of ABV: reading it

  • Right-skewed: most reviews are of beers between 4% and 8%, with a long tail past 10%
  • The maximum is 57.7%: Schorschbräu Schorschbock, a real freeze-distilled Eisbock, not a typo. An outlier is not automatically an error. Check before you drop

Task 6: Rating vs. Alcohol

First attempt: a scatter plot

Even with transparency. What is the problem, and what did you ask for instead?

Second attempt: average by ABV

Correlation: 0.40. Describe the relationship. Does stronger cause better?

Rating vs. alcohol: reading it

  • Ratings rise from about 11/20 at 3–4% to 15/20 at 9–10%, then flatten. Past 13% the points bounce: few beers live there (small dots)
  • Overplotting fix from Tuesday: 275,000 points became 16 averages
  • Causal? A 12% imperial stout is brewed with more care and reviewed by people who sought it out. “Stronger beers are rated higher” is a fact. “Alcohol makes beer better” is a story

What a strong answer looks like

The question (Task 6): Describe the relationship. Does it flatten? Does higher alcohol cause higher ratings?

Weak

“There is a positive relationship between ABV and rating. Correlation is not causation.”

Both sentences are true. Neither says anything about this data.

Strong

“Ratings climb from 11/20 at 4% to 15/20 at 10% (\(r\) = 0.40), then flatten; above 13% there are too few beers to say. I would not call this causal: strong beers are mostly imperial stouts and quadrupels, made with more ingredients and bought on purpose by enthusiasts, so style and reviewer both change with ABV.”

The recipe again: the pattern → the number → the reason → what it means for the decision.
