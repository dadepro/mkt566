A different kind of question

Every model so far had an outcome to predict: a listing’s price, then whether it is a gem. Today there is no outcome. There are just customers, described by numbers, and one question: who is similar to whom?

What changes

  • No \(Y\), no coefficients, no stars: the algorithm proposes groups
  • This is called unsupervised learning: nothing tells the algorithm what the right answer is

What stays

  • The data is still rows and columns, and you choose the columns
  • The output still means nothing until you interpret it
  • Still not causation: clusters describe, they do not explain

Why marketers cluster (and map)

Grouping similar customers is segmentation, and almost everything in marketing is segment-specific:

  • Messaging: bargain hunters get the discount email, service seekers get the concierge pitch
  • Product and pricing: which features matter to which group, and who will pay for them
  • Budget: which segments are growing, and where the next dollar goes
  • Positioning: the same kind of data, mapped, shows who competes with whom and where the gaps are: the perceptual map on the right

A sketch, not data. You will build a real one with PCA at the end of today.

Two tools today. K-means finds the groups. PCA draws the map you need to see them, and it powers the perceptual map.

Finding Groups: K-Means

Sixty customers, two numbers

Each dot is a customer: how often they bought, and how much they spent in total. How many groups do you see, and how would you describe each one?

The same chart, after k-means

  • We gave k-means only the numbers and k = 3. Where your eyes hesitated, it drew the borders by distance, the same way every time. Dots near a border could go either way: normal with real customers
  • The X marks each cluster’s center. Read the centers and the groups get names: occasional shoppers (about 6 purchases, $290), frequent small-basket shoppers (about 14 purchases, $560), and big spenders (about 16 purchases, $1,180)
  • Eyes can handle 2 columns and 60 customers, not 6 columns and thousands of customers. The algorithm can, with the same logic

How k-means works

You pick k, the number of clusters. The algorithm does the rest:

  1. Place k centers at random
  2. Assign every customer to the nearest center
  3. Move each center to the middle of the customers assigned to it
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until the centers stop moving
  • On the last chart, that is: drop three X’s at random, color every dot by its nearest X, move each X to the middle of its color, repeat. The X’s you saw are where they stopped
  • “Nearest” means plain distance between rows of numbers, so every column you feed it counts
  • The random start means results can vary from run to run. In practice, k-means is run many times (say, 20) from different starts, and the best result is kept

K-means never sees names, labels, or meanings. It groups rows of numbers by distance. Everything the clusters mean comes later, from you.

K-means, step by step

  1. Drop 3 centers at random
  2. Assign: each customer takes the color of its nearest X
  3. Move each X to the middle of its color
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until nobody switches

Each click runs one step. Before you click, guess: which customers will switch color?

Segmenting Office-Store Shoppers

A segmentation, step by step

Where to start, what to do, and why:

Step What to do Why
1. Choose the columns the six importance ratings they say what customers want, which the store can act on
2. Check the units standardize if the columns use different scales k-means measures distance, so big numbers would dominate
3. Choose k run k-means for k = 2 to 10, look for the elbow more clusters always fit better; the elbow shows when extra clusters stop paying off
4. Run k-means k = 3, from many random starts puts shoppers with similar ratings together
5. Profile the average rating of each cluster k-means only says 1, 2, 3; the averages say what each group wants
6. Name a short name for each cluster managers act on “bargain hunters”, not on “cluster 1”
7. Describe age, income, and job of each cluster who they are, and how to reach them

Step 1 of 7: the data, and which columns

40 respondents rated how important six store attributes are to them when buying office equipment, each on a 1–10 scale:

variety of choice, electronics, furniture, quality of service, low prices, and return policy

  • We also know three things about each respondent: professional (0/1), income (in $1,000s), and age
  • We cluster on the six ratings only: they say what customers want, and that is what the store can change (assortment, prices, service)
  • The descriptors say who customers are. We keep them out so that income or age cannot decide the groups: we want “price vs. service”, not “young vs. old”. They come back afterwards, to describe each group

Data from R for Marketing Students.

Step 2 of 7: check the units

Suppose you cluster on income ($15,000 to $70,000) and age (20 to 60):

  • Two customers $1,000 apart in income are 1,000 “units” apart; 25 years apart in age, only 25. The distance, and so the clusters, would be all income and no age
  • The fix: standardize first, replacing each column with z-scores (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation). Then every column counts equally

Standardize whenever the columns have different units. Today’s survey ratings all share the same 1–10 scale, so we can skip it, and keep the cluster averages in interpretable 1–10 units.

Step 3 of 7: choose k, the elbow curve

For each k, run k-means and add up the squared distances from every point to its cluster center: the “spread left inside the clusters”. Where does the curve bend?

Step 3 of 7: reading the elbow

  • Going from 2 to 3 clusters cuts the within-cluster spread from 636 to 288. From 3 to 4 saves only 36 more, and the curve flattens: the elbow is at k = 3
  • More clusters always fit better, so “best fit” cannot pick k: with one cluster per customer the fit is perfect and you have learned nothing
  • The elbow is a guide, not a law: k must also make business sense (three campaigns, not nine)

Steps 4 and 5 of 7: run k-means, profile the clusters

We ran k-means on the six ratings with k = 3, the elbow’s choice. It split the 40 respondents into clusters of 14, 18, and 8. The labels are just 1, 2, 3, in no particular order. The table shows each cluster’s average rating of each attribute (1 to 10):

Step 6 of 7: name the segments

We name each cluster by reading its row in the profile table (last slide): which attributes it rates high, and which it rates low.

Cluster (n) Rates high Rates low Our name, and why

Step 7 of 7: who is in each segment?

The three columns we kept out of the clustering describe who landed in each segment:

Segment Professional Income Age
Bargain hunters (14) 50% $32,000 31

Because these columns played no part in forming the clusters, the differences across segments are a finding, not something we built in.

Six Dimensions on One Screen: PCA

Why PCA?

We already have the clusters and their averages. Why do anything else?

  • We cannot see six ratings. With two columns (the 60 customers) our eyes could check what k-means did. With six ratings we cannot draw the shoppers, so we cannot check whether the clusters really stand apart
  • Many ratings say the same thing. Shoppers who care about variety also care about electronics and furniture (furniture and electronics move together: correlation 0.69). Three columns, one idea: assortment. PCA finds the few big ideas behind many ratings
  • Managers think in maps. A two-axis map shows who is close to whom, customers or brands, and where the gaps are

PCA turns six ratings into a two-axis map that keeps most of the information. We use it to see the segments, and to find the few dimensions customers really differ on.

PCA: six ratings, one map

  • PCA replaces the six ratings with new axes, called PC1, PC2, and so on. Each new axis is a mix of the six ratings, the way a GPA is a mix of grades
  • The axes come in order: PC1 captures the biggest differences between shoppers, PC2 the next biggest, and so on
  • Keep the first two, and every shopper gets a spot on a map

You do not need to know how the computer finds the axes. You need to answer two questions: how many axes to keep, and what each axis means.

How many axes to keep?

  • Each bar says how much of the differences between shoppers one axis keeps. The six bars add up to 100% (give or take rounding)
  • PC1 keeps 52% and PC2 26%: together 78%. After PC2 the bars drop to 9% and below
  • The rule: keep axes until the bars get small (an elbow, like the one in segmentation step 3), and check that the ones you keep add up to most of the total

Two axes keep 78% here, so the map tells most of the story. If they kept, say, 40%, the map would hide more than it shows.

What do the two axes mean?

Each arrow is one rating. It points to where shoppers who rate it high end up on the map:

  • PC1 (left vs. right): three arrows point left: variety, electronics, and furniture. Name the axis by what the three share: left means “wants a big assortment”. “Furniture” alone would leave out the other two
  • PC2 (up vs. down): low prices reaches highest, quality of service reaches lowest. Up means “cares about price, not service”; down, the reverse

PCA could just as well have flipped left and right, or up and down. What matters is which arrows point the same way, and which point opposite ways.

The map: 40 shoppers, colored by cluster

Each dot is a shopper, placed by PCA. The colors are the k-means clusters.

PCA drew the map; k-means picked the colors. The two never talked to each other.

Do the colors form regions?

The map: reading it

  • The clusters sit in separate regions, each at the tip of the arrows its profile predicted: bargain hunters by low prices, one-stop shoppers by variety and furniture, service seekers by quality of service
  • Why this matters: k-means always returns groups (ask for 3, you get 3), even when there are none. PCA never saw the clusters. If the colors still form separate clouds on its map, the groups are real. If they were mixed together, k-means would just have cut one blob into pieces

Perceptual maps answer positioning questions: who are my real competitors (neighbors on the map), what do I own (my nearest arrows), and where is the gap nobody occupies.

Three cautions for both methods:

  • Standardize when the columns use different units
  • Clusters and axes mean nothing until you read them: the averages for clusters, the arrows for axes
  • Clusters describe customers; they do not explain them. Service seekers are older on average, but that does not mean age makes people want service. It only tells us who tends to be in the group

From clustering to PCA: the steps, and why

Step What to do Why
1. Pick the columns what customers want (the ratings); standardize if the scales differ groups built on needs, with fair distances
2. Find the groups choose k with the elbow, then run k-means shoppers with similar ratings end up together
3. Read the means the average rating of each cluster, then name each one what each group wants, in exact numbers
4. Describe age, income, and job of each cluster who they are, and how to reach them
5. Check the axes run PCA; keep two axes if they hold most of the differences a map is only useful if it tells most of the story
6. Map and compare plot the shoppers on PC1 and PC2, color them by cluster, add the arrows see whether the groups really separate, and what separates them

Steps 3 and 6 check each other. The means give exact numbers, one attribute at a time; the map shows all the shoppers at once. The check: each cluster should sit next to the arrows of what it rates highest. Here it does (bargain hunters, who rate low prices 8.3, sit by the low-prices arrow), so the segments are real, not something k-means made up.
