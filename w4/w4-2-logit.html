A Yes/No Outcome

Our outcome: is a listing a “gem”?

An investor wants listings that are both well rated and actually booked. Define a gem as a listing with a rating of 4.5 or more and more than 20 reviews:

airbnb[, gem := as.integer(star_rating >= 4.5 & reviews_count > 20)]
table(airbnb$gem)

    0     1 
40460 10358 
mean(airbnb$gem)
[1] 0.2038254
  • gem is a dummy: 1 for a gem, 0 otherwise. About 20% of listings are gems
  • The question: what makes a listing more likely to be a gem? Start with price

The & means both conditions must hold; as.integer() turns TRUE/FALSE into 1/0, as in Task 7 of the case.

Share of gems by price

Same idea as the “average by group” charts of week 3, with a 0/1 variable: the average of a dummy is a share. What do you see?

Share of gems by price: reading it

  • Gems are rarer as price rises: 22–23% of listings between $50 and $150, 12% between $500 and $1,000, 6% above $1,000. The very cheapest bin is also lower
  • So the probability of being a gem depends on price. Can we model that probability with a regression, the way we modeled price on Thursday?

Try the tool we have: a straight line

lpm <- lm(gem ~ price, data = airbnb)   # OLS, with a 0/1 outcome
  • The “linear probability model”: the line is the predicted probability of being a gem
  • Intercept 0.217: a free listing has a 21.7% chance. Slope −0.00009: each extra dollar lowers it by 0.009 percentage points; $100 more, by about 0.9 points
  • Reasonable so far. Now look at the right end of the line

Where the line breaks

pred <- predict(lpm)
sum(pred < 0)          # listings with a predicted probability below zero
[1] 22
round(min(pred), 2)    # the lowest one
[1] -0.7
  • The line crosses zero at a price of about $2,372 and keeps going: a $10,000 listing gets a “probability” of −70%
  • A probability is trapped between 0 and 1. As it approaches either edge, the true relationship must flatten. A straight line cannot flatten: it falls at the same rate forever
  • The slope has the same flaw: the line takes 0.9 points off for every $100, whether a listing’s chance of being a gem is at 20% (a cheap listing) or at 2% (a very expensive one). At 2% there are only 2 points left to lose, so near the edge the effect of price must shrink

For a yes/no outcome we need a curve that is linear in the middle and flattens at both ends. That curve is the logistic function, and the model built on it is the logit (logistic regression).

Logistic Regression

The fix: squash the line into 0–1

Two steps, and only the second one is new:

  1. Keep Thursday’s straight line, but treat its output as a score, not as the probability: \(z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\). A score can be any number: 0.3, 12, \(-70\)
  2. Squash the score into 0–1 with the logistic function: \(p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\). Any score in, a probability out
  • A huge negative score becomes a probability near 0, never below it; a huge positive one, near 1, never above
  • You will never compute this by hand: R does. Your job is to read what comes out

Odds and log-odds

The odds are the chance it happens, divided by the chance it does not. Sports betting already speaks this language:

Probability Odds \(= p / (1 - p)\) In words
\(p = 20\%\) (the chance a random listing is a gem) \(0.2 / 0.8 = 0.25\) one to four: 1 gem for every 4 non-gems
\(p = 50\%\) \(0.5 / 0.5 = 1\) even: as likely as not
\(p = 80\%\) \(0.8 / 0.2 = 4\) four to one: 4 gems for every non-gem
  • Rearranging the S-curve puts the model on this scale: \(\dfrac{p}{1-p} = e^{z}\), and taking logs, \(\log\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right) = z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\)
  • The right-hand side is Thursday’s straight line. That is what “logit” means, and why everything from OLS carries over: predictors, dummies, base levels, standard errors, stars

A logit coefficient \(\beta_1\) is the change in the log-odds for one more unit of \(X\). Nobody thinks in log-odds, so the next slide converts.

Reading a logit coefficient

The rule: one more unit of \(X\) multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_1}\); R computes it for you (exp(coef(m))). The worked example previews the model we run next, a logit of gem on price: its coefficient will come out at \(\beta_1 = -0.0008\) per dollar. Your job is three steps:

Step Price example: \(\beta_1 = -0.0008\) per dollar
1. Sign and stars, as in OLS negative, significant: pricier listings are less likely to be gems
2. Scale: multiply \(\beta_1\) by the change you care about for $100 more: \(100 \times -0.0008 = -0.08\)
3. Convert: odds are multiplied by \(e^{\beta_1}\); the percent change is \(e^{\beta_1} - 1\) \(e^{-0.08} = 0.923\): $100 more multiplies the odds by 0.923, and \(0.923 - 1 = -0.077\): a 7.7% drop in the odds
  • Shortcut, as in Thursday’s log-level model: while the scaled coefficient is within about \(\pm 0.2\), just move the decimal (\(-0.08 \to\) about \(-8\%\)); bigger than that, use exp()

The percent change is in the odds, not in the probability. To turn odds back into a probability: \(p = \text{odds} \,/\, (1 + \text{odds})\). So a 50% increase in odds takes a probability of 20% to 27%, not to 30%: odds of \(0.25\) grow to \(0.375\), and \(p = 0.375 / 1.375 = 27\%\).

What a manager wants is the predicted probability: the chance that a listing at a given price is a gem. predict(lg1, type = "response") computes it (the score, squashed) for any price you name: a $50 listing has a 21.5% chance, a $200 one 19.6%, $500 16%, $1,000 11%, $5,000 0.5%. That is the blue curve on the previous slide, read off point by point.

How it is estimated, and how to judge the fit

Estimation. OLS picks the line with the smallest squared errors. Logit picks the coefficients that make the observed 0s and 1s most likely (maximum likelihood). R does it in a fraction of a second; you never need the formula.

Log-likelihood. How likely the data is under the model. Always negative; closer to zero is better. Use it to compare models on the same data, the way you used R² on Thursday.

There is no R². Alternatives:

  • Pseudo-R² (McFadden): how much the log-likelihood improves over a model with no predictors. 0 to 1, but values of 0.1–0.3 are already decent
  • Accuracy: predict “gem” when \(p > 0.5\) and count the hits. Beware the base rate: saying “not a gem” for everyone is right 80% of the time here. Week 9 (classifiers) covers this properly

For the price-only model: log-likelihood −25,650 vs. −25,697 without price; pseudo-R² 0.002. Price matters, and explains very little, the same lesson as Thursday.

From odds to probabilities

Odds ratios compare. Probabilities decide. predict() with type = "response" gives the probability for any listing you describe:

examples <- data.frame(price = c(100, 100, 300, 100), guests_included = 2,
                       city = c("Austin", "Boston", "Austin", "Austin"),
                       room_type = c("Entire home", "Entire home", "Entire home", "Shared room"))
examples$p_gem <- round(predict(m_logit, examples, type = "response"), 3)
examples
  price guests_included   city   room_type p_gem
1   100               2 Austin Entire home 0.208
2   100               2 Boston Entire home 0.236
3   300               2 Austin Entire home 0.157
4   100               2 Austin Shared room 0.114
  • An entire home in Austin at $100 for 2 guests: 21% chance of being a gem. The same listing in Boston: 24%. At $300 instead of $100: 16%. As a shared room: 11%
  • This is the sentence for the investor: “moving the same property from Austin to Boston raises the chance it becomes a gem from 21% to 24%”

Odds ratios for a table, probabilities for a decision. Always compute a few predicted probabilities for realistic cases before you write a recommendation.

Still not causation

Cheaper listings are more likely to be gems. Three stories, as always:

  1. Lower prices bring more bookings, hence more reviews, hence gem status
  2. Hosts of listings that are already popular have no need to discount, and hosts of struggling ones cut prices: the causality runs the other way
  3. Gems are older listings (20+ reviews take time), and older listings were priced in an earlier, cheaper market

“A $100 higher price is associated with 16% lower odds of being a gem.” The model holds fixed guests, city, and room type. It does not hold fixed listing age, photos, or host effort.
