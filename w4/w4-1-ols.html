We asked R for a straight line through the points. What line is it? What could a manager do with it?

A regression is the formula for that line

\[\text{sales} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{youtube}\]

  • A regression predicts one variable, \(Y\), from one or more others, \(X\)
  • \(Y\) is the outcome (or dependent variable). \(X\) is the predictor (or independent variable)
  • Examples:
    • Sales (\(Y\)) as a function of ad spend (\(X\))
    • Revenue (\(Y\)) as a function of review ratings (\(X\))
    • Airbnb price (\(Y\)) as a function of the number of bedrooms (\(X\))
    • Income (\(Y\)) as a function of years of education (\(X\))

In general \(Y = F(X)\): \(Y\) depends on \(X\) somehow. A linear regression assumes the simplest \(F\): a straight line. Machine learning (week 10) is mostly about learning fancier \(F\)’s.

The setup

For each row \(i\) of the data (each campaign):

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i\]

  • \(x_i\): the predictor (YouTube spend of campaign \(i\))
  • \(y_i\): the outcome (sales of campaign \(i\))
  • \(\beta_0\): the intercept, predicted \(Y\) when \(X = 0\)
  • \(\beta_1\): the slope, how much \(Y\) changes when \(X\) goes up by one unit
  • \(\epsilon_i\): the error, the part of \(y_i\) the line does not explain

The three pieces, on the chart

Every point has its own error: the vertical distance to the line. The intercept and the slope are the same for everyone.

Which line?

Both lines run through the points. The blue segments are the errors. Which line is better, and how would you decide?

Which line: reading it

  • Square each error (so misses above and below the line both count), add them up. Smaller is better
  • Ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the intercept and slope with the smallest sum of squared errors. Line B is that line, and it is what geom_smooth(method = "lm") drew last week
  • “Least squares” is the whole idea. Everything else today is about reading what comes out

The sum of squared errors, written out

For any intercept \(\beta_0\) and slope \(\beta_1\), the line predicts \(\hat{y}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) and misses by \(\epsilon_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\):

\[\text{SSE}(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \epsilon_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\right)^2\]

  • Every choice of \((\beta_0, \beta_1)\) gives a different line, hence a different SSE. Line A and Line B are two such choices
  • OLS picks the pair that makes this sum as small as possible:

\[(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1) = \arg\min_{\beta_0,\, \beta_1} \; \text{SSE}(\beta_0, \beta_1)\]

“Least squares” means exactly this: square each error, add them up, and pick the line that makes the total smallest.

Reading the output: the estimates

coef(model)
  (Intercept)       youtube 
8439.11225895    0.04753664
  • Intercept \(\beta_0 = 8{,}439\): a campaign with no YouTube spend is predicted to sell about 8,400 units
  • Slope \(\beta_1 = 0.0475\): each additional $1 on YouTube is associated with 0.0475 more units sold
  • Prediction: with $100,000 on YouTube, \(8{,}439 + 0.0475 \times 100{,}000 \approx 13{,}200\) units

Always translate the slope into business units: “one more unit of \(X\) goes with \(\beta_1\) more units of \(Y\)”. If you cannot say it in a sentence a manager understands, you have not read the coefficient yet.

The estimate comes with uncertainty

Our slope comes from these 200 campaigns. Another 200 would give a slightly different slope. The standard error (SE) measures how much it would move around.

round(coef(summary(model)), 4)
             Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept) 8439.1123   549.4115 15.3603        0
youtube        0.0475     0.0027 17.6676        0
  • Std. Error of the slope: 0.0027. The slope is pinned down to roughly \(0.0475 \pm 0.005\)
  • t value = estimate / SE = \(0.0475 / 0.0027 \approx\) 17.7: the slope is about 18 standard errors away from zero
  • Pr(>|t|) is the p-value: here so small that it rounds to 0 (two slides back, summary() printed it as <2e-16)

The question the p-value answers

Hypothesis testing is a way to decide between two claims with data:

  • \(H_0\) (the null): no relationship, \(\beta_1 = 0\). YouTube spend has nothing to do with sales
  • \(H_1\) (the alternative): there is one, \(\beta_1 \neq 0\)

The p-value answers: if \(H_0\) were true, how likely is a slope at least this far from zero, just by luck?

Decision rule

  • p-value < 0.05: reject \(H_0\). The coefficient is statistically significant (different from zero)
  • p-value ≥ 0.05: fail to reject \(H_0\). Not enough evidence

R’s stars: *** p < 0.001, ** p < 0.01, * p < 0.05, . p < 0.1

Smaller SE → larger t → smaller p → stronger evidence against “no relationship”

For YouTube, p is essentially 0: if spend and sales were unrelated, a slope of 0.0475 would almost never show up. So we are confident the relationship is real.

“Significant” is not the same as “big”

Two separate questions about every coefficient:

  1. Is it different from zero? That is what the p-value answers. It depends on the size of the estimate and on how much data you have
  2. Does it matter? That is about the size of the estimate, in business units. The p-value says nothing about it

With 50,000 rows (Airbnb, later today) almost everything is “significant”, including effects too small to care about. With 18 rows almost nothing is, including effects that are real. Read the size first, then the stars.

How good is the line? R-squared

Forty of the 200 campaigns. Without \(X\), the best guess for every campaign is the average. How much do the errors shrink once we use the line?

R-squared: reading it

\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{sum of squared errors with the line}}{\text{sum of squared errors with the mean}} = 1 - \frac{3,027,644,040}{7,800,694,200} = 0.61\]

  • Multiple R-squared: 0.61. YouTube spend explains 61% of the variation in sales across campaigns. The other 39% is other channels, seasonality, luck
  • \(R^2\) runs from 0 (the line is no better than the mean) to 1 (every point on the line)
  • Residual standard error: 3,910. The typical prediction misses by about 3,900 units. Same idea as \(R^2\), but in the units of \(Y\)
  • Adjusted R-squared is \(R^2\) with a small penalty for every predictor you add. Use it to compare models with different numbers of predictors

A low \(R^2\) does not make a coefficient wrong. It says that \(X\) alone leaves most of \(Y\) unexplained. Whether that matters depends on your goal: prediction needs a high \(R^2\), understanding one relationship does not.

The output, decoded

In the output What it is How to read it
Estimate \(\hat\beta\), the coefficient “One more unit of \(X\) goes with \(\hat\beta\) more units of \(Y\)”
Std. Error Precision of the estimate Smaller is better. Would the estimate move much with new data?
t value Estimate / SE How many SEs from zero. Beyond ±2 is “significant”
Pr(>|t|) p-value Chance of a t this large if the true coefficient is 0
Residual standard error Typical size of the error In units of \(Y\)
R-squared Share of the variation in \(Y\) explained 0 to 1
F-statistic Do the predictors explain anything together? Tiny p-value: yes

Interpreting the Coefficient

What does \(\beta_1\) tell us?

All else equal (ceteris paribus), how much \(Y\) changes when \(X\) moves by one unit. What “one unit” means depends on how you wrote the model:

Model Equation Reading of \(\beta_1\)
Level-level \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\) One more unit of \(X\) → \(\beta_1\) more units of \(Y\)
Log-level \(\ln Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\) One more unit of \(X\) → about \(100 \times \beta_1\) percent more \(Y\)
Level-log \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln X\) 1% more \(X\) → about \(\beta_1 / 100\) more units of \(Y\)
Log-log \(\ln Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln X\) 1% more \(X\) → about \(\beta_1\) percent more \(Y\) (an elasticity)

The percentage readings are approximations that work when \(\beta_1\) is small (say below 0.2). The exact formula for log-level is \((e^{\beta_1} - 1) \times 100\%\).

Why take logs?

1. Skewed variables. Price, income, sales, number of reviews: many small values, a few huge ones. You saw in week 2 that a log scale makes such distributions readable. The same is true for a regression: without the log, the few huge values pull the line around.

2. Multiplicative relationships. A $1 price increase means something very different at $5 and at $100. In logs, the model works in percentages, which is how the relationship usually behaves.

3. Interpretation. Managers think in percentages: “a 10% price increase lowers demand by 8%”. A log-log coefficient is that number, the elasticity.

Rule of thumb: log a variable that is positive and right-skewed (money, counts). Leave ratings, shares, and dummies as they are.

Logs change the question you are asking, not just the fit. Level: “how many more units?”. Log: “what percent more?”. Choose the one your manager needs.

Three ways: reading it

  • Level-level (column 1): $1 more on YouTube → 0.0475 more units
  • Log-level (column 2): $1 more on YouTube → about 0.0003% more sales (\(100 \times 0.000003\))
  • Log-log (column 3): 1% more YouTube spend → 0.36% more sales. That is the elasticity of sales with respect to YouTube spend

stargazer() puts several models side by side, with standard errors in parentheses and stars for significance. This is the table format for Homework 2 and for your project. Column labels, a title, and readable variable names are the difference between a table and a wall of numbers.

Elasticities below 1 mean \(Y\) responds less than proportionally: doubling YouTube spend does not double sales.

More than one predictor

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{k,i} + \epsilon_i\]

Everything we said still applies, with one change in the reading: each \(\beta_j\) is the change in \(Y\) for one more unit of \(x_j\), holding the other predictors fixed.

In R, add predictors to the formula with +:

m_all <- lm(sales ~ youtube + facebook + newspaper, data = marketing)

Before you look: last week’s charts showed YouTube strong, Facebook noisier, newspaper flat. Which coefficient do you expect to be largest? Which one close to zero?

More than one predictor: reading it

  • Facebook has the largest coefficient: $1 more on Facebook, holding YouTube and newspaper fixed, goes with about 0.19 more units. YouTube: 0.046 units per $1
  • Newspaper: \(-0.001\), p-value 0.86. Once YouTube and Facebook are in the model, newspaper spend tells us nothing about sales. Remember last week’s flat newspaper panel
  • \(R^2\) goes from 0.61 to 0.90: three channels explain 90% of the variation in sales

“Holding the others fixed” is what makes multiple regression useful. Campaigns that spend more on newspaper also spend more on Facebook (correlation 0.35). The single-predictor regression of sales on newspaper would give newspaper credit for Facebook’s work.

Regression Is Still Not Causation

What the coefficient does and does not say

Price is $37 lower in Los Angeles than in Austin. Reviews go with slightly lower prices. Facebook spend goes with more sales. All of these are associations. None of them tells you what happens if you change \(X\).

  • A regression holds fixed only the predictors you put in. Everything you left out (listing quality, neighborhood, season) is still free to move with \(X\) and \(Y\) at the same time
  • Adding predictors helps (newspaper’s “effect” vanished once Facebook was in). It does not settle the question. Weeks 11–12 are about designs that do

Words matter. Write “a one-unit increase in \(X\) is associated with a \(\beta_1\) change in \(Y\)”, not “\(X\) causes \(Y\)”, unless you have a reason to believe it, and can say why.
