A different kind of question

Every model so far had an outcome to predict: price on Thursday, gem on Tuesday. Today there is no outcome. There are just customers, described by numbers, and one question: who is similar to whom?

What changes

  • No \(Y\), no coefficients, no stars: the algorithm proposes groups
  • This is called unsupervised learning: nothing tells the algorithm what the right answer is

What stays

  • The data is still rows and columns, and you choose the columns
  • The output still means nothing until you interpret it
  • Still not causation: clusters describe, they do not explain

Why marketers cluster

Grouping similar customers is segmentation, and almost everything in marketing is segment-specific:

  • Messaging: bargain hunters get the discount email, service seekers get the concierge pitch
  • Product and pricing: which features matter to which group, and who will pay for them
  • Budget: which segments are growing, and where the next dollar goes

Two tools today. K-means finds the groups. PCA draws the map you need to see them, and it powers the classic marketing perceptual map along the way.

Finding Groups: K-Means

Sixty customers, two numbers

Each dot is a customer: how often they bought, and how much they spent in total. How many groups do you see, and how would you describe each one?

The same chart, after k-means

  • We told k-means only two things: the numbers and k = 3, the number of groups to find. It found the same groups your eyes did
  • The X marks each cluster’s center. The groups have obvious names: occasional shoppers, frequent small-basket shoppers, big spenders
  • Eyes work with 2 columns and 60 customers. With 6 columns and thousands of customers they do not. The algorithm scales; the logic stays the same

How k-means works

You pick k, the number of clusters. The algorithm does the rest:

  1. Place k centers at random
  2. Assign every customer to the nearest center
  3. Move each center to the middle of the customers assigned to it
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until the centers stop moving
  • “Nearest” means plain distance between rows of numbers, so every column you feed it counts
  • The random start means results can vary run to run: set.seed() makes them reproducible, and nstart = 20 runs it 20 times and keeps the best

K-means never sees names, labels, or meanings. It groups rows of numbers by distance. Everything the clusters mean comes later, from you.

Watch the units: distance and scale

Suppose you cluster on income ($15,000 to $70,000) and age (20 to 60):

  • Two customers $1,000 apart in income are 1,000 “units” apart; 25 years apart in age, only 25. The distance, and so the clusters, would be all income and no age
  • The fix: standardize first, replacing each column with z-scores (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation). In R: scale(data). Then every column counts equally

Standardize whenever the columns have different units. Today’s survey ratings all share the same 1–10 scale, so we can skip it, and keep the cluster averages in interpretable 1–10 units.

How many clusters? The elbow curve

For each k, run k-means and add up the squared distances from every point to its cluster center: the “spread left inside the clusters”. Where does the curve bend?

The elbow: reading it

  • Going from 2 to 3 clusters cuts the within-cluster spread from 636 to 288. From 3 to 4 saves only 36 more, and the curve flattens: the elbow is at k = 3
  • More clusters always fit better, so “best fit” cannot pick k: with one cluster per customer the fit is perfect and you have learned nothing
  • The elbow is a guide, not a law: k must also make business sense (three campaigns, not nine)

Segmenting Office-Store Shoppers

The data: an importance survey

40 respondents rated how important six store attributes are to them when buying office equipment, each on a 1–10 scale:

variety_of_choice, electronics, furniture, quality_of_service, low_prices, return_policy

  • We also know three things about each respondent: professional (0/1), income (in $1,000s), and age
  • We cluster on the six ratings only. The three descriptors stay out, and describe the segments afterwards
  • The file is Excel, so a new reader: read_excel() from the readxl package (fread() is for CSVs)

Data from R for Marketing Students. Script: w5-1-clustering-class.R, data: data/segmentation_office.xlsx, both in w5-code.zip.

K-means in R: one function

dd <- as.data.table(read_excel("data/segmentation_office.xlsx", sheet = "SegmentationData"))
ratings <- dd[, .(variety_of_choice, electronics, furniture,
                  quality_of_service, low_prices, return_policy)]
set.seed(123)                                     # random starts, reproducible
km <- kmeans(ratings, centers = 3, nstart = 20)   # k = 3, keep the best of 20 runs
km$size                                           # respondents per cluster
[1] 14 18  8
dd[, cluster := as.factor(km$cluster)]            # attach the labels: 1, 2, or 3
  • Three clusters of 14, 18, and 8 respondents. Each respondent now carries a cluster label
  • The labels are just 1, 2, 3, in no particular order. What they mean is the next slide’s job

Who is in each segment?

The three columns we kept out of the clustering describe who landed in each segment:

Segment Professional Income Age
Bargain hunters (14) 50% $32,000 31
One-stop shoppers (18) 33% $48,000 44
Service seekers (8) 75% $48,000 49
  • Bargain hunters are the young, lower-income group; service seekers are older professionals; one-stop shoppers sit in between with the largest headcount
  • This is the actionable half: ratings say what to offer each segment, descriptors say who and where they are

Because these columns played no part in forming the clusters, the differences across segments are a finding, not something we built in.

Six Dimensions on One Screen: PCA

The problem, and the squash

The toy chart worked because there were two columns. Our shoppers live in six. A screen has two axes; you cannot plot six.

  • PCA (principal component analysis) squashes the six columns into two new axes, chosen so the map keeps as much of the respondents’ variation as possible (Tuesday we squashed a score into a probability; today we squash six columns into two axes)
  • Each respondent gets a coordinate on each new axis: PC1 and PC2. Plot those two and you have the best two-axis picture of the data
pca <- prcomp(ratings, center = TRUE, scale. = TRUE)
round(summary(pca)$importance[2, ], 2)   # share of variation per component
 PC1  PC2  PC3  PC4  PC5  PC6 
0.52 0.26 0.09 0.05 0.04 0.03
  • PC1 keeps 52% of the variation, PC2 another 26%: the 2D map shows 78% of what six columns contain. Good enough to look at

What do the two axes mean?

Each arrow is one of the six ratings; its direction shows where that rating pulls a respondent on the map:

  • Left: variety, electronics, furniture. The left side of the map is “wants a big assortment”
  • Upper right: low prices, return policy. Lower right: quality of service
  • So PC1 separates assortment from price and service; PC2 separates price from service

The signs are arbitrary (R could have flipped left and right). Directions and groupings are what matter.

The map: 40 shoppers, colored by cluster

PCA drew the map; k-means picked the colors. The two never talked to each other. Do the colors form regions?

The map: reading it

  • The clusters occupy separate regions, and each sits at the tip of the arrows its profile predicted: bargain hunters by low prices, one-stop shoppers by variety and furniture, service seekers by quality of service
  • Two independent tools agree: the segments are in the data, not an artifact of one algorithm

The same trick on brands: a perceptual map

Same PCA, new rows: brands instead of shoppers, rated on six attributes. Which brands compete head-to-head? Who owns which attribute?

The perceptual map: reading it

  • OfficeStar sits alone on the right, with service, product quality, large choice, and, crucially, the preference arrow: the strong brand people like best
  • Supermarket is its mirror image: far left, near low prices and convenience, far from quality and preference. PaperNCo owns the cheap corner; OfficeEquipment competes on convenience with no clear strength
  • Two axes hold 98% of the variation here, so this map is essentially the whole story

Perceptual maps answer positioning questions: who are my real competitors (neighbors on the map), what do I own (my nearest arrows), and where is the gap nobody occupies. Script: w5-1-pca-class.R.

Clustering and PCA, side by side

K-means PCA
Question who is similar to whom? which directions matter?
Output a group label per row (1, 2, 3) two new columns (PC1, PC2) per row
You choose the columns, and k the columns
Read it via the profile table the loadings (arrows)
Marketing use segmentation perceptual maps, visualization
Together PCA draws the map, k-means draws the borders

Cautions that apply to both: standardize when units differ; the labels and axes mean nothing until you read them off a table; and clusters describe customers, they do not explain them. “Service seekers are older” is a pattern, not a mechanism.
