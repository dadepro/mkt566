Last week’s promise

Last Tuesday the neural network slide said: in marketing, the fancy models shine on text and images. This week we make good on the text half.

  • Reviews, tweets, chat transcripts, survey comments: most of what customers tell you arrives as text, not as a spreadsheet
  • Every model we have used this semester (regression, logit, trees, forests) eats rows and columns of numbers
  • So the whole game today is one move: turn text into numbers without losing what it says. Once that is done, Thursday’s prediction step is machinery you already know

Why marketers care about text

  • Text reveals what customers think (perception), what happened to them (experience), and how they feel about it (emotion), in their own words
  • What marketing teams do with it: track brand health across millions of posts, find the product flaw behind a wave of one-star reviews, route angry support tickets first, detect fake reviews
  • The scale is the point: no team can read a million reviews, but a model can score all of them in minutes

This is not a new kind of analytics. It is the same pipeline as always, with one extra step at the front: text in, numbers out, then the usual models.

From Text to Numbers

The pipeline, start to finish

Step What it does Example
1. Clean lowercase, strip punctuation and HTML “Great movie!” becomes “great movie”
2. Tokenize split the text into words “great movie” becomes [“great”, “movie”]
3. Drop stopwords remove glue words that carry no meaning “the”, “a”, “and” go away
4. Count one row per document, one column per word the bag-of-words matrix
5. Weight up-weight distinctive words TF-IDF
6. Model feed the numbers to a model the logit from week 5, Thursday

Steps 1 to 5 are new today. Step 6 is not new: once text is a matrix of numbers, everything from weeks 4 to 9 applies unchanged.

Our running example: 5,000 movie reviews

Real data, loaded live: the movie_review dataset that ships with the text2vec R package. 5,000 IMDB reviews, each labeled positive or negative (2,517 positive, 2,483 negative).

Two real examples, shortened:

“the classic war of the worlds” by timothy hines is a very entertaining film that obviously goes to great effort and lengths to faithfully recreate h. g. wells’ classic book. mr. hines succeeds in doing so. i, and those … (positive)

this movie could have been very good, but comes up way short. cheesy special effects and so-so acting. i could have looked past that if the story wasn’t so lousy. if there was more of a background story, it would have be… (negative)

After cleaning, these 5,000 reviews contain about 1.2 million words drawn from 41,891 distinct terms. Nobody reads that; the pipeline turns it into a table.

Steps 1 and 2: clean, then tokenize

One sentence, worked through:

Stage Result
Raw Great movie!! <br /> A must-see... 10/10
Lowercase great movie!! <br /> a must-see... 10/10
Strip HTML and punctuation great movie a must see 10 10
Tokenize (split on spaces) great · movie · a · must · see · 10 · 10
  • Each piece is a token. From here on, the model never sees sentences, only tokens and their counts
  • Real text is dirty: these reviews contain leftover HTML like <br />, which would otherwise become a nonsense token. Look at your tokens before you model them

N-grams: when one word is not enough

Splitting into single words destroys word order, and sometimes the order is the meaning:

  • “good” and “not good” say opposite things, but as single tokens they both contribute the word good
  • The fix: also count pairs of consecutive words, called bigrams: not_good becomes its own token, distinct from good
  • Single words are unigrams; you can go to trigrams and beyond, but the number of distinct terms explodes, so most marketing work stops at bigrams

The most common words

Every word in all 5,000 reviews, counted. What do you notice about the words at the top, and how useful would they be for telling a good review from a bad one?

Stopwords: the glue gets removed

  • The raw top 15 was all the, a, and, of: grammar glue. These words appear in every document, so they cannot separate anything from anything
  • Words like these are called stopwords, and step 3 simply deletes them from a standard list (the list is a choice: in some problems “not” matters, so check before deleting it)
  • After removal, the top words are movie, film, story, time: now the counts start to mean something

Bag-of-words: the counting step

Take a tiny, made-up corpus of three phone reviews, and count every word in every document:

  • A: “great phone great camera”
  • B: “terrible battery terrible screen”
  • C: “great battery”
doc battery camera great phone screen terrible
A 0 1 2 1 0 0
B 1 0 0 0 1 2
C 1 0 1 0 0 0
  • This table is the bag-of-words matrix: one row per document, one column per word, counts in the cells. Review A is the vector (0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0)
  • “Bag” because order is thrown away: “great phone” and “phone great” give the same row. That is the price of turning text into a table

Bag-of-words at real scale

On the real corpus, the same table is 5,000 rows by 41,891 columns, one column for every distinct term.

  • Almost every cell is zero: a 200-word review touches 200 of the 41,891 columns. This is called a sparse matrix, and R stores only the non-zero cells
  • Simple and interpretable: every column is a word you can read
  • Two problems remain: common words get big counts just for being common, and 41,891 columns is far more columns than we have reviews. The first problem we fix now; the second is Thursday’s whole topic

TF-IDF: up-weight the words that identify a document

A word matters for a document when it is common in that document but rare everywhere else. TF-IDF scores exactly that, in two parts:

  • TF (term frequency): how much of document \(d\) is word \(t\)? \(\text{TF}(t,d) = \frac{\text{count of } t \text{ in } d}{\text{words in } d}\)
  • IDF (inverse document frequency): how rare is the word across documents? \(\text{IDF}(t) = \log\frac{N}{\text{docs containing } t}\), with \(N\) the number of documents. A word in every document gets \(\log(1) = 0\): no weight at all
  • TF-IDF \(= \text{TF} \times \text{IDF}\): big only when both parts are big

TF-IDF by hand, on the toy corpus

For document A (“great phone great camera”, 4 words) with \(N = 3\) documents:

word count in A TF = count/4 docs with word IDF = log(3/df) TF-IDF
camera 1 0.25 1 1.10 0.275
phone 1 0.25 1 1.10 0.275
great 2 0.50 2 0.41 0.203
  • “great” appears twice in A, but it is also in 2 of the 3 documents, so its IDF is low: TF-IDF = 0.203
  • “phone” appears once, but only A has it: TF-IDF = 0.275, the highest score
  • That is the whole idea: “great” is everywhere; “phone” tells you which review this is. The model should listen to “phone” more

Which words separate good reviews from bad ones

For every word, we counted how often positive reviews use it and how often negative reviews use it (per 10,000 words), then took the ratio. “worst” at 8x on the left means negative reviews use it 8 times more often than positive ones. What do you see?

Reading the chart

  • Negative reviews lean on worst, waste, awful, stupid: direct complaint words. Positive reviews lean on excellent, wonderful, perfect, amazing
  • Nobody told the data which words are “good”: the labels plus simple counting surfaced them. This is exactly the signal Thursday’s model will learn from
  • The two sides are not symmetric: the sharpest negative word tilts about 8x, the sharpest positive one only about 4x. Complaints use extreme words; praise words also show up inside negative reviews (“the trailer was great, the movie was not”). No single word decides a review, which is why the model adds up thousands of small signals

Word embeddings, in plain words

Bag-of-words has one blind spot left: to it, “great” and “awesome” are as unrelated as “great” and “battery”: different columns, nothing shared.

  • Embeddings fix that by giving every word a list of, say, 300 numbers (a vector), learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors: awesome ends up close to great, far from battery
  • They are learned from huge text collections by models like word2vec (guess the word from its neighbors), and you download them pretrained rather than training your own
  • The trade-off by now is familiar: embeddings capture meaning but the 300 numbers are unreadable; counts are readable but treat every word as an island

Three ways to turn text into numbers

Method Each document becomes Strength Weakness
Bag-of-words counts over the vocabulary simple, readable common words dominate; synonyms unrelated
TF-IDF weighted counts highlights distinctive words; the workhorse still ignores meaning and order
Embeddings an average of word vectors captures meaning (great is close to awesome) unreadable numbers; needs a pretrained model

Default advice for marketing problems: start with TF-IDF. It is fast, transparent, and with a linear model it is a strong baseline. Reach for embeddings when synonyms clearly matter and you have the labels to prove the gain. Thursday we test exactly this on our 5,000 reviews.

Measuring Opinion: Sentiment

Sentiment: the first thing marketers ask of text

  • Sentiment is the polarity of an opinion: positive, negative, or neutral. “Is this review happy or angry?”
  • Where it earns money: track customer satisfaction without surveys, watch a campaign land in real time, spot a brand crisis while it is still small, prioritize the angriest support tickets
  • Two ways to measure it, and they differ in what they need: a dictionary or labeled data

Way 1: a dictionary of charged words

Lexicon-based sentiment (the sentimentr package in R):

  • Start from a dictionary: thousands of words pre-scored by humans (great = +0.75, terrible = -0.75, table = 0)
  • Score a sentence by averaging the scores of its words
  • The good packages also handle valence shifters: “not great” flips the sign, “absolutely terrible” amplifies it. This is why they work at the sentence level, not word by word

No training data needed: you can score a million reviews on day one. The price: the dictionary does not know your domain (“thin” is bad for a laptop battery and good for a laptop).

sentimentr, live on four sentences

Sentence Sentiment score
This movie is great. 0.25
This movie is not great. -0.22
Absolutely terrible, I want my money back. -0.28
The plot is slow at first, but the ending is fantastic. 0.13
  • Sentence 1 vs. sentence 2: the “not” flips a clearly positive score to a negative one. A plain word count would have scored both as positive, because both contain “great”
  • Sentence 3: “absolutely” amplifies “terrible”, the most negative score of the four
  • Sentence 4 is the honest case: mixed opinions inside one review average out to a mildly positive score. Real reviews are full of this, which is why sentence-level scoring beats word counting

Beyond positive vs. negative

Two directions you should know exist, both one Google away:

  • Emotion detection: dictionaries that map words to emotions rather than polarity (the NRC lexicon tags ~14,000 words with joy, anger, fear, trust…). “Which campaign made people excited, and which made them anxious?”
  • Topic modeling (LDA): unsupervised grouping of documents by the words they share, clustering for text. “What are the five things people complain about?” It finds the themes; you name them, exactly like the customer segments in week 6

What text analysis will not tell you

  • Sarcasm and context: “Great, it broke after two days” scores positive on words. Dictionaries fall for it; trained models only partly escape it
  • The domain shifts: a dictionary built on movie reviews mis-scores banking complaints. Re-check whenever you change domains
  • Volume is not representativeness: the people who write reviews are not your average customers (week 1’s Groupon paper all over again)
  • Correlation is not causation, still: angry reviews predict churn; that does not mean the words cause it
