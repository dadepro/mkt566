Too Many Columns

The matrix is wider than it is tall

Build Tuesday’s TF-IDF matrix on the training reviews and count:

  • 4,000 training reviews (80% of the 5,000; the other 1,000 are locked away as the test set)
  • The training reviews contain 37,936 distinct words. First cleanup: drop every word seen fewer than 10 times; you cannot learn what “ferrari” means for sentiment from 3 examples. That leaves 6,731 word columns
  • A logit estimates one coefficient per column: 6,731 coefficients from 4,000 examples. Still almost two knobs per example

Week 5’s logit was built for a handful of columns. Nothing in it says what to do when the columns outnumber the rows. Something has to give.

What actually goes wrong: memorizing

We ran that logit, with no guardrails, on the 6,731-column matrix. Here is its report card:

Training reviews Test reviews (never seen)
Accuracy 100% 83%
AUC 1.000 0.909
  • 100% accuracy on the training set: the model found enough rare-word combinations to label every training review perfectly, like a student who memorizes past exams instead of learning the material
  • On new reviews it drops to 83%. The gap between the two columns is overfitting, week 9’s trap at full scale
  • And it used 2,073 words to do it: far too many to read or trust

The Fix: Regularization

The idea, in plain words

Make the model pay a price for every coefficient it uses. Instead of minimizing only its mistakes, it now minimizes:

\[\text{Loss} \;=\; \text{how wrong the predictions are} \;+\; \lambda \times \text{how big the coefficients are}\]

  • Week 5’s logit minimized only the first term; the second term is the new penalty
  • A word now earns a coefficient only if it improves predictions enough to justify its price. Rare coincidences (“ferrari” appeared in 3 positive reviews) are no longer worth paying for
  • λ (lambda) sets the price. λ = 0: no penalty, the memorizing logit from the last slide. λ huge: every coefficient gets forced to zero and the model predicts the same probability for everyone
  • This is called regularization, and the R package that does it for the logit is glmnet

The formula, symbol by symbol

\[\text{Loss} \;=\; \text{DataFit} \;+\; \lambda \left[\, \alpha \,\|\beta\|_1 \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\|\beta\|_2^2 \,\right]\]

Symbol Name In plain words
DataFit the logit’s loss how wrong the predictions are on the training data (what week 5’s glm minimized)
\(\beta\) the coefficients one per word: how much that word moves the score
\(\|\beta\|_1\) sum of absolute values the lasso penalty: every nonzero coefficient costs
\(\|\beta\|_2^2\) sum of squares the ridge penalty: big coefficients cost extra
\(\lambda\) penalty strength the price dial: 0 = free, huge = everything forced to zero
\(\alpha\) the mix 1 = pure lasso, 0 = pure ridge, in between = elastic net

Two dials to set, and only two: α (which penalty) and λ (how strong).

Dial 1, α: lasso or ridge?

Lasso (α = 1) Ridge (α = 0) Elastic net (0 < α < 1)
What it does to coefficients pushes many to exactly zero shrinks all toward zero, none exactly a mix of both
What you get a short list of words that matter every word keeps a small say a compromise
Pick it when you believe few features carry the signal you believe many features each matter a little you are not sure (α = 0.5 is a common default)
  • Lasso’s exact zeros are the headline: it does feature selection for free. Out of thousands of words, it hands you the few hundred worth keeping, and you can read them
  • Today we use lasso (α = 1): we expect a modest set of opinion words to carry most of the signal, and we want the readable shortlist

Dial 2, λ: do not guess, cross-validate

  • λ = 0 memorizes; λ = huge predicts the base rate for everyone. The right value is somewhere in between, and it differs for every dataset, so nobody picks it by hand
  • The tool you already have: cross-validation (week 9). Try a grid of λ values; for each one, do 5-fold CV on the training set and record the average AUC; pick the λ that scores best
  • In R this is one line: cv.glmnet(X, y, family = "binomial", type.measure = "auc", nfolds = 5, alpha = 1) tries about 100 values of λ and does all the folding for you

The test set is not touched during any of this. It stays in the vault until the very end, exactly like week 9.

The setup, step by step

Everything below ran live when this deck was built (the class script has the code):

Step What we did Guardrail
1. Split 80% train (4,000), 20% test (1,000), seed 123 test set in the vault
2. Tokenize lowercase, split into words same as Tuesday

Note how many of the guardrails are week 9 rules, not new text rules. Text changed the features; the discipline is unchanged.

The CV curve

One point per candidate λ: the average AUC over the 5 folds (grey band: ± one standard error). What happens at the two ends, and where would you set the dial?

Reading the CV curve

  • Left edge (λ near zero): almost no penalty, the model memorizes, and the cross-validated AUC suffers. Right edge (heavy λ): the model is forced to drop everything and AUC collapses toward 0.5, a coin flip
  • The top of the curve is the sweet spot. cv.glmnet marks two choices: lambda.min (blue), the λ with the best average AUC, and lambda.1se (gold), the heaviest penalty still within one standard error of the best: a “just as good, but simpler” option
  • The sizes here: lambda.min keeps 492 words; lambda.1se keeps only 277. We use lambda.min today

The scoreboard

All four scored on the same 1,000 test reviews none of them saw:

Model Needs labeled data? AUC Accuracy
Coin flip no 0.500 50%
Tuesday’s dictionary (sentimentr) no 0.824 71%
Logit, no penalty (λ ≈ 0) yes 0.909 83%
Lasso at lambda.min yes 0.927 85%
  • Training on labels buys about 14 points of accuracy over the dictionary; that is the price of the labels, paid back
  • The penalty beats no penalty on every metric and uses 492 words instead of 2,073: better and smaller

The words the model trusts

The lasso’s largest surviving coefficients. A review’s score is, at heart, the sum of the coefficients of the words it contains. What do you recognize from Tuesday?

Reading the words

  • These are Tuesday’s counting-chart words again (worst, awful, waste vs. great, perfect, excellent), but this time learned from the labels, each with a weight, jointly, accounting for all other words
  • Of the 6,731 words, the lasso kept 492 and set the other 6,239 to exactly zero. What survives is a shortlist a manager can read and sanity-check

The code: glm vs. glmnet

Week 5, the plain logit

m <- glm(y ~ x1 + x2,
         data = train,
         family = "binomial")
  • a formula and a data frame
  • fine for a handful of columns
  • one model back

This week, the regularized logit

cv <- cv.glmnet(X_train, y_train,
                family = "binomial",
                type.measure = "auc",
                nfolds = 5, alpha = 1)
p <- predict(cv, X_test,
             s = "lambda.min",
             type = "response")
  • a matrix (sparse is fine), not a formula
  • built for thousands of columns
  • a whole path of models, one per λ, with CV built in

The recipe, reusable

Text classification, end to end:

  1. Get labeled examples (positive/negative, spam/not, churned/stayed)
  2. Split train/test; the test set goes in the vault
  3. Clean, tokenize, build the vocabulary on the training set, prune rare terms
  4. TF-IDF, fit on train, applied to both
  5. cv.glmnet(..., family = "binomial", type.measure = "auc")
  6. Evaluate on the test set; pick the threshold the business needs (week 9)
  7. Read the surviving words: do they make sense?

The same seven steps run spam filters, support-ticket routers, and fake-review detectors. Only step 1 changes.

The Game

The sentiment game

Teams, laptops out: w10-sentiment-analysis-game.R (also in w10-code.zip). Run the top of the script once, then call run_tfidf_experiment(dt, ...) with your own settings. One twist: in the game the labels come from Tuesday’s dictionary (that is how teams label text when nobody has labeled it for them).

Knob What it changes
ngram_max = 2 add bigrams: “not_good” becomes its own feature
remove_rare, term_count_min drop rare words (we used 10 today)
remove_popular, doc_proportion_max drop words that are in almost every review
l2_norm normalize each review’s TF-IDF row
alpha 1 = lasso, 0 = ridge, between = elastic net
use_cv = TRUE / lambda_no_cv CV-chosen λ vs. a λ you set by hand

The rules: do not touch the seed or the split. Highest test AUC wins; ties go to the smaller model. You have 25 minutes, then every team reports its best settings and we discuss why they won.
